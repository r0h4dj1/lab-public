# Idea to Design Process

A lightweight, structured approach to taking a product idea from concept to a
design ready for build. It replaces endless brainstorming with disciplined
convergence—each phase strips away ambiguity before committing design resources.

## 1. JTBD Statement

**Write and rewrite until it's precise.**

Jobs to Be Done (Christensen/Moesta) reframes your idea from "what it does" to
"what job users hire it for." Before designing anything, write the JTBD
statement: _"When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can
[expected outcome]."_ This single sentence should govern every design decision.

## 2. Assumption Audit

**List all assumptions. AI stress-tests them. Rank by risk.**

Before designing anything, write down every assumption your idea rests on:
market size, user behaviour, technical feasibility, willingness to pay. Then use
AI to argue against each one. This is faster and often more honest than real
user research at the idea stage. The goal isn't to kill the idea—it's to find
the 2-3 riskiest assumptions to validate first.

## 3. Wardley Sketch

**20 minutes mapping what to build vs. buy.**

Wardley Mapping is a strategic planning technique that maps your idea's
components on two axes: value chain (from user need down to infrastructure) and
evolution (from genesis to commodity). It's brilliant for solo devs because it
shows you what to build vs. what to buy/use off-the-shelf, and where you'll face
genuine uncertainty vs. solved problems.

## 4. Shape Up Pitch

**1-2 pages: problem, appetite, sketch, rabbit holes, no-gos.**

Shape Up (Basecamp) is especially suited to solo developers. Its core idea is
fixed appetite (time you're willing to spend) rather than fixed scope. You write
a pitch—covering problem, appetite, solution sketch, rabbit holes, and
no-gos—before any execution begins. This is probably the best starting point for
a solo dev.

## 5. Spec-First PRD

**AI-assisted, iterated until design decisions feel obvious.**

Write a full product requirements document before any design or code. A good
spec covers: problem statement, user stories, functional requirements,
out-of-scope decisions, success metrics, and key design constraints. The
discipline of writing it forces clarity; AI accelerates iteration. Once you have
a spec, your design decisions become much more constrained and tractable.

## 6. Crazy 8s

**8 rough UI sketches in 8 minutes, forces divergent thinking before committing.**

Divergent sketching works best when you don't yet know how to solve the
problem—but it can survive late in the process, only if scoped tightly to UI
layout and interaction pattern rather than to the underlying concept. At that
stage you know what you're building—you don't know how it should feel to use it.
A constrained sketch session asking "8 ways to present this feature" is
meaningfully different from the full divergent version.
